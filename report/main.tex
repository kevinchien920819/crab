\section*{Abstract}

This report studies an adaptation of \crab{} (Contrastive Representation and
Multimodal Aligned Bottleneck) from speech emotion recognition to audio deepfake
detection. The original speech-text architecture is reformulated as a
bona fide/spoof classifier by combining self-supervised speech representations
with a rhythm branch extracted from forced-alignment-based duration features.
Experiments on ASVspoof 5 compare audio-only, audio-rhythm, and audio-text
variants with different encoders and contrastive supervision settings. The
results show that the audio-only Transformer provides the strongest baseline in
this report, while rhythm fusion and MPCL require further refinement.

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} audio deepfake detection, ASVspoof, self-supervised speech representation, contrastive learning, \crab

\vspace{1em}

\section{INTRODUCTION}

Recent neural text-to-speech (TTS) and voice conversion (VC) systems can generate highly natural and speaker-similar speech~\cite{yi2023audio}.
As a result, audio deepfake detection (ADD), which aims to distinguish bona fide speech from spoofed or synthetic speech, has become an important problem in speech processing and information security.
Reliable ADD systems are especially important because synthetic speech can be used to imitate a speaker's identity, bypass voice-based authentication, or spread misleading audio content.

In recent years, self-supervised learning (SSL) models have become important front-end components for speech-related tasks because they can learn rich representations from large amounts of unlabeled data.
Models such as wav2vec 2.0~\cite{baevski2020wav2vec2}, HuBERT~\cite{hsu2021hubert}, and WavLM~\cite{chen2022wavlm} have shown strong performance across various downstream tasks, including automatic speech recognition, speaker verification, emotion recognition, and spoofing detection.
For ADD, these SSL representations provide strong acoustic cues, but synthetic speech may also contain unnatural temporal patterns that are not fully described by frame-level spectral representations alone.
For example, the duration of syllables, vowels, and consonants, as well as the rhythm between neighboring speech units, may reveal differences between human speech production and generated speech~\cite{warren2025pitch,attorresi2022combining,wang2023crossdataset}.

This report studies whether such rhythm information can be incorporated into the \crab{} framework.
The original \crab{} model was designed for speech emotion recognition (SER), where speech and text representations are aligned through cross-modal attention and supervised at multiple intermediate layers using Multi Layer Contrastive Supervision (MLCS).
In this work, we adapt the same representation-learning idea to ADD by replacing the original text branch with a rhythm branch extracted from forced-alignment outputs.
The goal is to make the adapted architecture and its ablation behavior clear: the experiments compare audio-only, audio-rhythm, and audio-text variants, different sequence encoders, and the effect of contrastive supervision.


The main objectives and contributions of this report are summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We adapt the \crab{} framework from speech emotion recognition (SER) to audio deepfake detection (ADD), reformulating the original emotion classification task as bona fide/spoof classification.
    \item We construct a syllable-level rhythm branch from forced-alignment outputs, where each utterance is represented by duration-based tokens derived from syllable, vowel, and consonant intervals.
    \item We describe an audio-rhythm multimodal architecture that encodes SSL speech representations and rhythm representations separately, then aligns them through cross-modal attention.
    \item We conduct ablation experiments over modality choice, encoder type, and multi-layer contrastive supervision to clarify the role of each component in the adapted detection system.
\end{enumerate}

The rest of this report is organized as follows.
Section 2 reviews related work on prosody-based ADD and the original \crab{} model.
Section 3 describes the proposed methodology, including prosody preprocessing, the rhythm encoder, and the audio-rhythm adaptation of \crab{} for ADD\@.
Section 4 presents the experimental setup for the SER reproduction and ASVspoof 5 ADD experiments.
Section 5 reports the SER reproduction results, ADD ablation results, and analysis.
Section 6 concludes the report.



\section{RELATED WORK}
\subsection{Prosody Features for Audio Deepfake Detection}
Early prosody-based methods mainly rely on handcrafted or explicitly extracted prosodic descriptors.
These approaches commonly use \(F_0\)-based statistics, jitter, shimmer, harmonics-to-noise ratio (HNR), rhythm, pitch, accent-related cues, and phoneme-duration information to characterize unnatural prosodic patterns in synthetic speech~\cite{warren2025pitch,attorresi2022combining,wang2023crossdataset}.
In particular, Pitch Imperfect~\cite{warren2025pitch} evaluates acoustic-prosodic deepfake detection with mean and standard deviation of \(F_0\), jitter, shimmer, and mean and standard deviation of HNR\@.
Other feature-fusion methods combine prosody with speaker verification embeddings, pronunciation features, and wav2vec representations to improve synthetic speech detection and cross-dataset generalization~\cite{attorresi2022combining,wang2023crossdataset}.

More recent work incorporates prosody directly into representation learning and training objectives. HuLA~\cite{mahapatra2025hula} uses \(F_0\) prediction and voiced/unvoiced classification as auxiliary tasks so that a self-supervised speech backbone can learn natural prosodic variation, improving robustness to expressive, emotional, and cross-lingual synthetic speech.
ProSDD~\cite{mahapatra2026prosdd} further models speaker-conditioned prosodic variation through pitch, voice activity, and energy, and learns transferable prosodic representations via supervised masked prediction.

\subsection{\crab{} model}
The \crab{} model was first proposed for speech emotion recognition (SER), where speech and text inputs are jointly modeled by a bimodal Cross-Modal Transformer~\cite{ueda2026crab}.
Its architecture uses WavLM to extract frame-level speech representations and RoBERTa~\cite{liu2019roberta} to extract token-level text representations.
After modality-specific projection and recurrent encoding, cross-modal attention aligns the two streams, and attention pooling converts them into utterance-level representations for emotion classification.

The central training idea in \crab{} is Multi Layer Contrastive Supervision (MLCS), which adds contrastive supervision to several intermediate representations instead of applying supervision only at the final classifier.
Each supervised layer is connected to a Contrastive Supervision Leg (CSL), a small projection head that maps the intermediate representation into a contrastive embedding space.
The CSL is trained with Multi Positive Contrastive Learning (MPCL), where samples with the same emotion label are treated as positives and samples with different labels are treated as negatives.
For an anchor embedding \(a\) and candidate embeddings \(\{b_i\}_{i=1}^{K}\), MPCL first defines a similarity distribution
\[
    q_i =
    \frac{\exp(a \cdot b_i / \tau)}
    {\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp(a \cdot b_j / \tau)},
\]
where \(\tau\) is a temperature parameter and embeddings are \(\ell_2\)-normalized.
The target distribution assigns probability mass only to candidates that share the same label as the anchor:
\[
    c_i =
    \frac{\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{match}}(a,b_i)}
    {\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{match}}(a,b_j)}.
\]
The MPCL loss is the cross-entropy between these two distributions,
\[
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MPCL}}
    = -\sum_{i=1}^{K} c_i \log q_i.
\]
Compared with ordinary supervised contrastive learning, this formulation explicitly represents all same-class candidates as multiple positives in a categorical target distribution.
In MLCS, the final SER objective combines the standard classification loss with the average MPCL loss from all selected CSLs:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ser}}
    =
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(m_K(x), y)
    +
    \alpha \frac{1}{|\Omega|}
    \sum_{i\in\Omega}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MPCL}}(m_i(x), y),
\]
where \(\Omega\) denotes the set of layers with CSLs, and \(\alpha\) controls the strength of contrastive supervision.
This design encourages modality-specific, cross-modal, and fused representations to become discriminative before the final prediction layer, which is the part of \crab{} most relevant to our adaptation for audio deepfake detection.

To obtain reliable temporal units for prosodic feature extraction,
we first preprocess each utterance through a three-stage pipeline consisting of transcription, forced alignment, and syllable boundary generation.
Figure~\ref{fig:preprocess} illustrates the overall process flow-chart of prosody feature preprocessing.
In the transcription stage, WhisperX~\cite{bain23interspeech} is applied to the raw audio signals to generate utterance-level transcripts.
These transcripts serve as the textual input for the subsequent alignment procedure.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figure/preprocess.png}
    \caption{Overview of the preprocessing pipeline.}
    \label{fig:preprocess}
\end{figure}

Given the audio-transcript pairs, we then employ the Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA) to obtain fine-grained temporal alignments~\cite{mfa_english_us_arpa_acoustic_2024}.

Before alignment, the generated transcripts are scanned for out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words with respect to the pronunciation dictionary.
If OOV entries are detected, a grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) model is used to predict their pronunciations, and the pronunciation dictionary is updated accordingly.
This OOV detection and dictionary expansion process is repeated until all transcript tokens can be covered by the dictionary.

After the dictionary covers all transcript tokens, MFA is used to align the speech signal with the normalized transcript, producing a TextGrid file that contains word- and phone-level timing information.

Finally, the TextGrid output is passed to our syllabification module, which converts the phone-level alignment into syllable-level boundaries.
These syllable boundaries provide the temporal anchors required for extracting prosodic descriptors, such as duration-related features and syllable-level rhythmic patterns.
Through this preprocessing procedure, each ASVspoof5 utterance is transformed into a structured representation with transcript, phonetic alignment, and syllable timing information for downstream prosody analysis.

\subsection{Prosody Encoder}
Following the rhythm encoder design of Liu and Lu~\cite{liu2024tone}, we encode
prosodic timing as a sequence of duration tokens. The rhythm encoder represents
each valid syllable position using three interval types:
\[
\mathcal{S}=\{\mathrm{SYL},\mathrm{V},\mathrm{CONS}\},
\]
where \(\mathrm{SYL}\) denotes the whole syllable interval, 
\(\mathrm{V}\) denotes the vocalic interval,
and \(\mathrm{CONS}\) denotes the consonant interval.
For interval type \(s\in\mathcal{S}\), \(\mathrm{Int}_{s,i}\) denotes the duration of the
\(i\)-th interval in an utterance with \(N\) valid syllable positions.

For each interval type, we compute three durational measurements. The first is
the raw duration of the interval, \(\mathrm{Int}_{s,i}\). The second is the
deviation of the current interval from the utterance-level mean duration of the
same interval type:
\[
\mathrm{Devi\mu}_{s,i}
=
\mathrm{Int}_{s,i}
-
\mu_{Int_{s,i}}
\]
The third is the local durational difference between consecutive intervals,
defined as
\[
\mu\mathrm{Diff}_{s,i} =
\frac{\mathrm{Int}_{s,i} - \mathrm{Int}_{s,i+1}}
{(\mathrm{Int}_{s,i} + \mathrm{Int}_{s,i+1})/2}
\]
At the \(n\)-th interval, \(\mu\mathrm{Diff}_{s,n}\) is replaced by
\(\mathrm{nPVI}\text{-}\mathrm{Int}\), i.e., the normalized pairwise
variability index for a specific type of speech interval, which measures
the speech rhythm of an utterance:
\[
\mu\mathrm{Diff}_{s,n}
=
\mathrm{nPVI}\text{-}\mathrm{Int}
=
\frac{
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}
\left|
\frac{\mathrm{Int}_{k}-\mathrm{Int}_{k+1}}
{(\mathrm{Int}_{k}+\mathrm{Int}_{k+1})/2}
\right|
}{n-1}.
\]

The resulting duration sequence is denoted as
\[
D = [d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{9 \times n},
\]
where each duration token \(d_i \in \mathbb{R}^9\) contains nine features that capture durational variability.
Each token is first linearly projected into a \(d_{model}\)-dimensional representation using a learnable projection weight
\(W_d \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{model} \times 9}\).
To preserve the temporal order of the sequence, sinusoidal positional encoding is then added to the projected duration embeddings, following the original Transformer~\cite{vaswani2017attention}.
Finally, the input embedding of the rhythm encoder is defined as
\[
e_i = LN\left(W_d \cdot d_i + p_i\right),
\]
where \(LN(\cdot)\) denotes layer normalization, and \(p_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{model}}\) is the positional encoding vector at position \(i\).

\subsection{\crab{} for ADD}
Figure~\ref{fig:proposed_model_architecture} shows the proposed adaptation of
\crab{} for audio deepfake detection. The original \crab{} model is designed
for SER and uses a speech-text Cross-Modal Transformer with WavLM and
RoBERTa~\cite{liu2019roberta} in the original \crab{} design~\cite{ueda2026crab}. In this work, we keep the main representation
learning principle of \crab{}, but reformulate the architecture for ADD\@. The
task label is changed from an emotion category to a binary deepfake label
\(y \in \{0,1\}\), where \(0\) denotes bona fide speech and \(1\) denotes spoofed
speech. 
More importantly, the text branch is replaced by a rhythm branch. Although
transcript or contextual information can be useful in some context-dependent ADD
settings, lexical content is not a consistently necessary or reliable cue for
spoof detection. In contrast, synthetic speech often exhibits acoustic and
prosodic artifacts in duration, syllable timing, pitch, and vowel-consonant
rhythm patterns. The resulting model is an audio-rhythm \crab{} network.


Given an input waveform \(x\), a self-supervised speech encoder first extracts
a frame-level acoustic representation \(S\):
\[
S = f_{\mathrm{ssl}}(x), \qquad
S \in \mathbb{R}^{F \times D_s}.
\]
Here, \(f_{\mathrm{ssl}}(\cdot)\) denotes the SSL speech encoder, \(F\) is the
number of speech frames, and \(D_s\) is the speech feature dimension. In this
report, the SSL encoder can be instantiated with wav2vec 2.0/XLS-R style
representations~\cite{baevski2020wav2vec2}. This branch provides the model with
frame-level acoustic information from the waveform.

In parallel, the duration sequence \(D\) produced by the prosody encoder is
converted into a rhythm representation \(R\):
\[
R = f_{\mathrm{rhythm}}(D), \qquad
R \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times D_r}.
\]
Here, \(f_{\mathrm{rhythm}}(\cdot)\) denotes the rhythm encoder, \(L\) is the
number of rhythm tokens, and \(D_r\) is the rhythm feature dimension. This branch
summarizes syllable-level timing information, so it describes how speech units
are distributed over time rather than what words are spoken.

Because the speech and rhythm representations have different feature dimensions,
they are projected into a shared hidden space before fusion:
\[
\tilde{S} = LN(W_s \cdot S), \qquad
\tilde{R} = LN(W_r \cdot R).
\]
In this equation, \(W_s\) and \(W_r\) are modality-specific projection matrices,
and \(LN(\cdot)\) denotes layer normalization. The projected sequences
\(\tilde{S}\) and \(\tilde{R}\) therefore have compatible hidden dimensions,
which allows the later cross-modal attention block to compare the two streams.

After projection, each modality is encoded by its own bidirectional GRU:
\[
H_s = \mathrm{BiGRU}_s(\tilde{S}), \qquad
H_r = \mathrm{BiGRU}_r(\tilde{R}).
\]
The resulting representations satisfy
\(H_s \in \mathbb{R}^{F \times 2h}\) and
\(H_r \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times 2h}\), where \(h\) is the hidden size of one GRU
direction. This step lets each branch model temporal context within its own
sequence before the two modalities interact.

The cross-modal block then aligns the two streams through multi-head attention.
The speech stream attends to the rhythm stream as follows:
\[
A_s = \mathrm{MHA}(Q=H_s, K=H_r, V=H_r).
\]
In this attention operation, speech features are used as queries, while rhythm
features are used as keys and values. The output \(A_s\) can be interpreted as
rhythm-aware speech information.

The reverse direction is computed in the same way, where rhythm features attend
to the speech stream:
\[
A_r = \mathrm{MHA}(Q=H_r, K=H_s, V=H_s).
\]
Here, rhythm features are used as queries, and speech features are used as keys
and values. The output \(A_r\) can be interpreted as speech-aware rhythm
information.

The attention outputs are then added back to their original unimodal
representations through residual connections:
\[
Z_s = H_s + A_s, \qquad Z_r = H_r + A_r.
\]
The residual design keeps the original speech and rhythm information while
injecting cross-modal context. As a result, \(Z_s\) is the speech representation
after rhythm alignment, and \(Z_r\) is the rhythm representation after speech
alignment.

Since the final prediction is made at the utterance level, attention pooling is
used to summarize each aligned sequence into one fixed-length vector:
\[
z_s = \mathrm{AttPool}(Z_s), \qquad
z_r = \mathrm{AttPool}(Z_r).
\]
Here, \(z_s\) and \(z_r\) are utterance-level speech and rhythm vectors,
respectively. Attention pooling allows the model to assign larger weights to
frames or rhythm tokens that are more informative for spoof detection.

The two utterance-level vectors are concatenated to form the final multimodal
representation:
\[
C = [z_s;z_r] \in \mathbb{R}^{4h}.
\]
The vector \(C\) contains information from both the acoustic stream and the
rhythm stream. It is then passed through the classifier block, which consists of
layer normalization, a fully connected layer, ReLU activation, dropout, and a
final fully connected layer that outputs two logits for bona fide and spoof
classes.

We also adapt Multi Layer Contrastive Supervision (MLCS) from the original
\crab{} model to the ADD setting. Each green-highlighted module in
Figure~\ref{fig:proposed_model_architecture} is connected to a Contrastive
Supervision Leg (CSL), implemented as an FC-ReLU-FC projection head. For the
\(i\)-th supervised layer, the CSL maps an intermediate representation
\(m_i(x)\) into a contrastive embedding \(u_i\):
\[
u_i = g_i(m_i(x)).
\]
In this equation, \(g_i(\cdot)\) is the projection head of the \(i\)-th CSL\@.
The MPCL loss is then computed using the ADD label: samples with the same bona
fide/spoof label are treated as positives, and samples with different labels are
treated as negatives. Therefore, MLCS encourages modality-specific, aligned, and
fused representations to become label-discriminative before the final classifier.

The overall training objective combines the ordinary classification loss with
the averaged contrastive loss from all selected CSLs:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{add}}
=
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(o,y)
+
\alpha \frac{1}{|\Omega|}
\sum_{i \in \Omega}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MPCL}}(u_i,y).
\]
Here, \(o\) denotes the classifier logits, \(y\) is the bona fide/spoof label,
\(\Omega\) is the set of layers with CSLs, and \(\alpha\) controls the strength
of contrastive supervision. During inference, the CSL branches are removed, and
prediction only uses the audio-rhythm fusion path and the final classifier.
This keeps inference simple while using MLCS during training to improve the
separability of bona fide and spoof representations.


\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{IEMOCAP}
We use IEMOCAP~\cite{busso2008iemocap} to reproduce the SER setting of the
original \crab{} model before adapting the framework to ADD\@. IEMOCAP contains
two-speaker conversations collected from five sessions, with utterance-level emotion
annotations and corresponding transcripts.

The IEMOCAP data collection mainly targets five emotion categories: neutral,
happiness, excitement, anger, and sadness. Following the common four-class SER
protocol, happiness and excitement are merged into the same positive emotion
class. The final label set is therefore
\(\{\mathrm{neutral}, \mathrm{happy/excited}, \mathrm{angry}, \mathrm{sad}\}\).
The dataset is split by speaker/session to avoid direct speaker leakage:
Session 5 is used as the test set, one actor from Session 4 is used as the
development set, and the remaining utterances are used for training.

For the reproduced SER experiment, WavLM Large~\cite{chen2022wavlm} is used as the speech encoder
and RoBERTa Base~\cite{liu2019roberta} is used as the text encoder. The model is trained for 20 epochs
with AdamW and a CosineAnnealingLR scheduler. We set the learning rate of the
SSL encoder and text encoder to \(1 \times 10^{-6}\), and the learning rate of
the SER classification head to \(1 \times 10^{-5}\). The batch size is set to 4
with gradient accumulation over 8 steps. The training objective combines
weighted cross-entropy with MPCL, and the best checkpoint is selected according
to development-set UAR\@. We report both WAR and UAR on the test set.

\subsection{ASVspoof 5 Dataset}
We use ASVspoof 5~\cite{wang2025asvspoof5designcollection} as a benchmark to evaluate the performance of our model.
A common practical preprocessing choice is to use a fixed-length strategy,
where each utterance is normalized to a 10-second segment by cropping longer audio and repeating or padding shorter audio.
Although this setting is practical for batch-based training, it may discard spoofing cues located outside the selected segment. 
In contrast, we process the entire utterance without fixed-duration cropping, allowing the model to exploit the full temporal information available in the ASVspoof 5 recordings.

Table~\ref{tab:asvspoof5_data} summarizes the ASVspoof 5 preprocessing results in this study.
 Models are trained on the training set and validated on the validation set to select the best checkpoint for testing.

Our proposed \crab{} for ADD models is trained with a learning rate of \(1 \times 10^{-5}\) using the AdamW optimizer,
without any data augmentation. The number of training epochs is set to 20, the batch size is set to 8,
and gradient accumulation is performed over 8 steps.

Table~\ref{tab:ser_results} shows that the re-implemented \crab{} model obtains
competitive performance on IEMOCAP, with a WAR of 0.7365 and a UAR of 0.7429.
Although it does not exactly match the reported \crab{} score, the result
suggests that the main SER architecture is reasonably reproduced and can be used
as the basis for the ADD adaptation.

Table~\ref{tab:deepfake_result} shows that the audio-only Transformer without
MPCL achieves the best ADD performance, with an EER of 4.81\%, a min-DCF of
0.1358, and an act-DCF of 0.1425. Adding rhythm features does not improve the
result in the current experiments, and MPCL is also not consistently helpful.
This does not necessarily mean that rhythm information is useless. A more likely
interpretation is that the present multimodal pipeline may still be affected by
implementation or preprocessing issues, such as alignment errors, syllable
boundary noise, padding and masking problems, modality imbalance, or loss
weighting. The pure-audio model has a simpler data path and is therefore less
exposed to these possible sources of error. For this reason, the ADD results
should be viewed as preliminary ablations that identify where the audio-rhythm
model needs further debugging and refinement.

\section{CONCLUSION}
This report adapted the \crab{} framework from speech emotion recognition to
audio deepfake detection by reformulating the task as bona fide/spoof
classification and replacing the original text branch with a rhythm branch. The
adapted model combines SSL speech representations, syllable-level duration
features, cross-modal attention, attention pooling, and Multi Layer Contrastive
Supervision to study audio-rhythm fusion for ADD.

The ablation results show that SSL-based audio representations remain a strong
baseline, with the audio-only Transformer setting achieving the best ADD
performance in this report. Adding rhythm features or MPCL does not consistently
improve performance, suggesting that rhythm information is sensitive to
alignment quality, feature design, and fusion strategy. Future work should
improve rhythm feature reliability and explore more robust multimodal fusion
before the audio-rhythm model can be considered fully optimized.
